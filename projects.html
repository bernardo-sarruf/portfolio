GLOBAL HYDROGEN PRODUCTION TECHNOLOGIES

The Global Hydrogen Production Technologies Center (HyPT) is a £14.1 million (5.2M£ UKRI), five-year international research programme established to accelerate the development of low-cost, net-zero hydrogen production technologies and support their deployment at scale. The Centre brings together researchers across the UK, US, Canada, Australia, Germany and Egypt, combining expertise across hydrogen production, materials, engineering, technology development and wider environmental and societal considerations. HyPT focuses on major technology pathways including water electrolysis, methane pyrolysis and photocatalytic solar water splitting, with the overarching objective of reducing the cost and improving the sustainability of hydrogen production.

At the University of Birmingham, the HyPT programme focuses particularly on the development of novel materials and manufacturing approaches for high-temperature electrolysis, with the aim of achieving very high efficiencies and enabling scalable hydrogen production.

As a Co-Investigator within the Birmingham team, I contribute to the development and scale-up of ceramic electrolyser technologies. My role combines project coordination, financial and resource oversight and hands-on experimental research. This includes materials development, electrochemical testing and the investigation of factors affecting long-term performance and durability. Through HyPT, I also work within a broader international research network, connecting Birmingham's expertise with complementary capabilities across academia and industry.

INNOVATE UK · UK–SOUTH KOREA COLLABORATION

HiDroConnect — High Durability Solid Oxide Electrolyser Stacks with Enhanced Coated Interconnects and Metal Ion Infiltrated Electrodes — is an Innovate UK-funded UK–South Korea collaborative R&D project running from 2023 to 2026. The project addresses key challenges limiting the long-term performance, durability and scale-up of solid oxide electrolysers (SOEs), with particular emphasis on metallic interconnects and electrode materials. Interconnects perform several critical functions within an SOE stack, including electrical current collection and separation of adjacent cells, but their operation at high temperature can lead to oxidation, increased electrical resistance and chromium evaporation. Chromium released from metallic interconnects can migrate towards the air electrode and contribute to performance degradation.

The project focuses on developing high-durability protective coatings for metallic interconnects, using physical vapour deposition (PVD) approaches that offer dense coatings and potential scalability towards commercial applications. The consortium includes, Teer Coatings Ltd., the University of Birmingham, E&KOA and Changwon National University.

I took responsibility for the University of Birmingham contribution in July 2026, after the project was already underway. My role is therefore focused on the ongoing experimental activities rather than the original proposal development. My main focus is the long-term assessment of protective coatings through area-specific resistance (ASR) measurements, supported by chromium evaporation studies to evaluate coating stability, electrical performance and chromium retention under conditions relevant to solid oxide electrolysis. Through this work, I contribute to understanding the durability and degradation of coated interconnects and their implications for the long-term performance of SOE stacks.

MARIE SKŁODOWSKA-CURIE ACTIONS · HORIZON 2020

flexell — Reversible Solid Oxide Cell Development for the Utilisation of Alternative Fuels and Hydrogen Strategic Production — was a Marie Skłodowska-Curie Actions project funded by the European Commission under Horizon 2020. The project ran from August 2021 to October 2023 and had a total project cost and EU contribution of €224,933.76. It was coordinated by the University of Birmingham and hosted within the Centre for Fuel Cells and Hydrogen Research.

The project aimed to develop a proof-of-concept reversible solid oxide cell capable of operating flexibly in both fuel-cell and electrolysis modes. The concept combined the conversion of liquid and gaseous fuels, including ethanol, methane and natural gas, into electricity with the ability to operate in electrolysis mode to produce hydrogen from steam and electricity. The project therefore addressed the use of solid oxide cells as flexible energy-conversion devices capable of responding to changing energy-system requirements.

As the Marie Skłodowska-Curie Research Fellow leading the flexell research, I focused on the development and optimisation of anode-supported reversible solid oxide cells, with particular emphasis on fuel flexibility and materials development. My research included the development of ceria-nickel fuel electrodes, reversible operation with alternative fuels such as ethanol and biogas, and electrolysis using dry and wet CO₂. The project also involved ceramic processing, materials characterisation, electrochemical testing and the development of approaches for scaling cell manufacturing. The work resulted in multiple peer-reviewed publications and provided the foundation for my subsequent research into reversible SOCs, degradation and durability.

INDUSTRIAL DECARBONISATION · SIM CONSULTING IN TECHNOLOGY

Hydrogen-Based Iron-Ore Enrichment

Through SIM Consulting in Technology, I led a Phase 1 feasibility and process-development study for hydrogen-based iron-ore enrichment through direct reduction. The project aimed to assess the technical and economic feasibility of increasing the iron content of hematite-rich ore using hydrogen or synthesis gas as alternatives to conventional carbon-based reduction, with the objective of reducing CO₂ emissions. The 2.5-month Phase 1 study combined literature review, process modelling, reactor design, techno-economic assessment and experimental planning.

Twelve scenarios were evaluated, considering different ore feedstocks, reducing gases and degrees of reduction, from hematite-to-magnetite enrichment to complete reduction to metallic iron. Reduction-kinetics modelling was used to estimate operating conditions, residence times and reactor dimensions, whilst CAPEX, OPEX and environmental impacts were assessed for each scenario.

The study identified partial reduction as the more practical initial development route because complete reduction required substantially greater infrastructure and operating costs. It also established a staged scale-up strategy, progressing from laboratory experiments through two prototype stages before pilot-scale development. The work culminated in the definition of the experimental reactor, equipment specifications and Phase 2 strategy, providing the technical and economic basis for subsequent experimental validation and technology development.
