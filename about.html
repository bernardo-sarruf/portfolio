ABOUT

A career shaped by hydrogen.

From early work in hydrogen research in Brazil to international research, teaching and technology development in the UK, my career has been built around connecting materials, electrochemistry and real-world energy challenges.

MY JOURNEY

From materials science to low-carbon hydrogen.

My professional journey began in 2008 at the Hydrogen Laboratory at COPPE/UFRJ in Brazil. What started as a materials engineering pathway developed into a long-term career in hydrogen technologies, spanning research, teaching, technology development, industrial consultancy and international collaboration.

Across more than 18 years, I have worked at the interface between materials science and electrochemistry, with a particular focus on solid oxide cells, alternative fuels, electrolysis, degradation and durability. My work has increasingly moved from understanding materials and cell performance towards developing technologies that are scalable, durable and relevant to industrial decarbonisation.

2008 — BRAZIL

The beginning: hydrogen and materials.

I began my hydrogen research at COPPE/UFRJ, progressing from undergraduate research into increasingly independent work in fuel cells, electrochemical materials and materials characterisation. This period established the scientific foundation that continues to shape my research today: understanding how materials structure, processing and chemistry determine electrochemical performance.

Alongside research, I developed experience in materials characterisation and engineering problem-solving, building the practical and analytical skills that would later become central to my work on solid oxide cells. I also designed and built bespoke test rigs tailored to accommodate specialised experimental requirements, including the use of ethanol and alternative fuels such as simulated biogas.

2018 — 2021

From research to academic independence.

After completing my doctoral research, I continued at UFRJ as a Postdoctoral Research Fellow, working on hydrogen technologies, fuel cells and electrochemical materials. I also became involved in teaching and academic supervision, contributing to materials science, phase transformations, materials characterisation and fuel-cell education.

During this period, I developed a broader research profile around solid oxide technologies and alternative fuels, whilst strengthening my ability to lead experiments, manage research activities and communicate complex engineering topics to different audiences.

My academic work was recognised with the 2018 CAPES Thesis Prize — Honours, following the completion of my doctoral research in Materials Engineering. I also served on the Scientific and Organising Committees for the World Hydrogen Energy Conference (WHEC 2018), held in Rio de Janeiro.

2020 — PRESENT

Research, teaching and international collaboration.

In 2020, I became a Collaborator Professor at UFRJ, combining research with undergraduate and postgraduate teaching and supervision. In 2021, I moved to the UK through a Marie Skłodowska-Curie Fellowship at the University of Birmingham, where I led research within the flexell project on reversible solid oxide cells and flexible operation with alternative fuels.

My work during this period explored ceria-based fuel electrodes, reversible operation with ethanol and biogas, steam and CO₂ electrolysis, and the relationship between materials design and long-term cell performance. The research resulted in peer-reviewed publications and established a foundation for my subsequent work on degradation, durability and scale-up.

2021 — PRESENT

Building an international hydrogen research profile.

Since joining the University of Birmingham, I have expanded my work from individual cell research towards larger collaborative programmes. As a Research Fellow and Co-Investigator in HyPT, I contribute to ceramic electrolyser development and scale-up through project coordination, resource oversight and hands-on experimental research.

I also lead the University of Birmingham contribution to HiDroConnect, working with industrial and international partners on protective coatings for solid oxide electrolyser interconnects. My experimental work includes long-term area-specific resistance measurements and chromium evaporation studies to assess coating performance and durability.

These projects have strengthened my experience of working across research disciplines, institutions and industry, whilst giving me a broader perspective on the challenges involved in moving hydrogen technologies towards practical deployment.

INTERNATIONAL ENGAGEMENT

Connecting research communities across borders.

International collaboration has become a defining part of my professional trajectory. Through European research programmes, consortium development and long-standing links with Brazilian research centres, I have built a network spanning academia, industry and hydrogen technology communities across Europe, Brazil, North America, Asia and Australia.

I have contributed to the development of international research proposals, evaluated MSCA Postdoctoral Fellowship applications and participated in expert activities supporting the definition of future Clean Hydrogen Joint Undertaking research topics. I use these connections not only to develop research collaborations, but also to create opportunities for students and early-career researchers to engage with international laboratories, researchers and industrial partners.

TODAY

From materials and cells to technology impact.

Today, my research sits at the intersection of materials science, electrochemistry and low-carbon hydrogen technology. My work spans solid oxide fuel cells and electrolysers, materials development, electrochemical performance, degradation and durability, circularity and industrial applications.

My long-term ambition is to develop durable and resource-efficient hydrogen technologies by connecting fundamental materials research with technology development and industrial decarbonisation. This means not only improving performance, but also understanding lifetime, resource use, recovery and the practical requirements of deployment.

Teaching remains equally important to my academic identity. I aim to bring current research, practical experimentation and my international network into the learning environment, helping students connect engineering fundamentals with the technologies and challenges shaping the energy transition.
